CH. 01 A field guide · from 127.0.0.1 to the cloud

What is localhost?

It's your computer, talking to itself. Every app you've ever built started here — on a private address that never leaves your machine. Here's how it really works, why it breaks, and how a request travels from localhost to a data center on the other side of the planet.

§ 01 The short answer

localhost, in one paragraph

Quick answer

localhost is the standard hostname for the computer you're using right now. Your operating system maps it to a special loopback address — 127.0.0.1 in IPv4, ::1 in IPv6 — so anything sent there turns around inside your machine instead of going out to the network.

Developers use it to run and test apps privately. It's also why nobody else can open your http://localhost:3000 link: on their device, localhost means their computer, not yours.

Zoom 010 m

Your computer

When you open http://localhost:3000, your browser and your server are two programs on the same machine. The request goes down your operating system's network stack, reaches the loopback interface — a network card made entirely of software — and comes straight back up to the program listening on port 3000.

No Wi-Fi, no router, no internet. That's why localhost is fast, private, and works on a plane.

Distance 0 mRound trip ~0.05 msReach only you
Zoom 0210 m

Your home network

Your router hands every device a private address — say 192.168.1.23 for your laptop and 192.168.1.42 for your phone. Your phone can open your app at http://192.168.1.23:3000, as long as your server listens on all interfaces (0.0.0.0) and not only on 127.0.0.1.

Type localhost on your phone and you'll reach… your phone. Every device has its own localhost.

Distance ~10 mRound trip ~1–5 msReach your Wi-Fi
Zoom 0310 km

Your internet provider

Your whole home usually shares one public IP address, held by your router. Requests going out are fine — that's how you browse. But unexpected connections coming in are dropped by default: that's NAT and your router's firewall doing their job.

So a friend across town can't open your laptop's app. There's no door from the internet into your living room — and that's a good thing.

Distance ~10 kmRound trip ~5–20 msReach still just your home
Zoom 0410,000 km

The internet

Your provider connects to thousands of other networks — together, they are the internet. Data crosses oceans through fiber-optic cables on the sea floor, as pulses of light moving at about 200,000 km per second.

Even at that speed, distance costs time. Loading this page, your browser just made a round trip to this site's server in ….

Distance ~10,000 kmRound trip ~20–250 msReach anyone, if a server answers
Zoom 05The cloud

A computer that never sleeps

A data center is a building full of always-on computers with public addresses, fast connections, backup power and industrial cooling. “The cloud” means renting a slice of one — by the month, the hour, or even the second.

Deploying is copying your app from your localhost onto one of those machines, so anyone, anywhere can reach it — even when your laptop is closed.

§ 03 Interactive

127.0.0.1 vs 0.0.0.0 vs the cloud: who can reach your server?

The address your server listens on decides who can talk to it. Flip between the three and watch the connections.

Your server listens on
Your browser: Workshttp://localhost:3000 — the request loops back inside your laptop.
Your phone: Connection refusedYour phone reaches your laptop, but the server only accepts connections from 127.0.0.1.
A friend: Can't get inYour home router has no rule to forward strangers to your laptop, so the connection never arrives.

Open your dev server to your phone (same Wi-Fi)

Most dev servers listen on 127.0.0.1 by default — a safe choice. To test on your phone, tell the server to listen on every interface, then open your laptop's LAN address on the phone, like http://192.168.1.23:5173.

Heads upListening on 0.0.0.0 lets every device on that network reach your server. Do it on networks you trust, and never with real secrets in a debug build.
Listen on all interfaces
# Vite (React, Vue, Svelte…) — prints a "Network:" URL
npm run dev -- --host

# Flask
flask run --host=0.0.0.0

# Django (also add your LAN IP to ALLOWED_HOSTS)
python manage.py runserver 0.0.0.0:8000

# Rails
bin/rails server -b 0.0.0.0

# Find your laptop's LAN address (macOS)
ipconfig getifaddr en0

§ 06 Try it yourself

Six experiments you can run in 60 seconds

Open a terminal. Each one reveals a piece of the machinery that's been quietly running on your computer all along.

Exp. 01 · Ping yourself

Measure a trip of zero meters

Watch your computer answer itself in a few hundredths of a millisecond — faster than any network on Earth.

macOS / Linux
ping -c 3 localhost
Exp. 02 · The address book

Find where “localhost” is defined

Your computer's hosts file maps names to addresses before any DNS server is asked. Look for 127.0.0.1 localhost.

macOS / Linux · Windows
cat /etc/hosts
# Windows:
type C:\Windows\System32\drivers\etc\hosts
Exp. 03 · Be a web server

Serve a folder in one line

Run this in any folder, then open http://localhost:8000. Congratulations — you're running a web server.

Any OS with Python 3
python3 -m http.server 8000
Exp. 04 · Infinite names

Every *.localhost is you

RFC 6761 reserves the whole .localhost domain for loopback. With the server above still running, open http://hello.localhost:8000 in Chrome.

macOS
open http://hello.localhost:8000
Exp. 05 · 16,777,216 of you

The whole 127.x.x.x block loops back

127.0.0.1 is just the famous one. On Linux, every address from 127.0.0.1 to 127.255.255.254 reaches your own machine. (macOS only answers on 127.0.0.1 unless you add more.)

Linux
ping -c 1 127.12.34.56
Exp. 06 · Open doors

See every port that's listening

List every program on your machine waiting for connections — the “doors” that localhost can knock on.

macOS / Linux · Windows
lsof -iTCP -sTCP:LISTEN -n -P
# Windows:
netstat -ano | findstr LISTENING

§ 08 FAQ

Frequently asked questions

Is localhost the same as 127.0.0.1?

Almost always, yes. localhost is a name; 127.0.0.1 is the IPv4 loopback address it normally resolves to. Many systems also map localhost to the IPv6 loopback address ::1. Either way, the request stays on your own computer.

What does localhost:3000 mean?

It means “the program listening on port 3000 on this computer.” localhost picks the machine and the number after the colon picks the program. Port 3000 is the default for tools like Next.js, Ruby on Rails and many Node.js tutorials. See every common localhost port.

Why does it say “localhost refused to connect”?

Your computer received the request, but no program was listening on that port, so the operating system refused the connection (ERR_CONNECTION_REFUSED). Usually the server isn't running, crashed, or is on a different port than the one in the URL. Here's the full troubleshooting guide.

Can other people visit my localhost website?

No. localhost always points to the device making the request, so a http://localhost:3000 link sent to a friend points at their own computer. To let anyone visit, deploy your app to a server with a public address — that's what people mean by putting it “in the cloud.”

Why does localhost work on my computer but not on my phone?

Because your phone has its own localhost. To reach your laptop from your phone on the same Wi-Fi, use the laptop's local network address (something like 192.168.1.23:3000) and make sure your dev server listens on 0.0.0.0, not only 127.0.0.1.

What is 0.0.0.0?

When a server listens on 0.0.0.0, it accepts connections on every network interface the machine has — loopback, Wi-Fi, Ethernet. It isn't an address you visit; it's an instruction meaning “all of them.”

Is localhost safe? Can hackers reach it?

Traffic to localhost never leaves your computer, and services that listen only on 127.0.0.1 can't be reached from other devices. One caveat: web pages you visit run code on your machine, so a malicious page could try to talk to your local servers. Modern browsers increasingly block that or ask for permission first.

How do I put my localhost site online?

Copy it to a computer that's always on and has a public address — a cloud server — and point a URL at it. Today you can ask an AI coding agent to do it in one sentence, or use a platform like Vercel, AWS or DigitalOcean. Compare the options in the deploy guide.
